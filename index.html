A plain, grey piece of fabric lays on the desk in front of me. Mostly cotton, I believe. I have never sewn anything ever. But here I am, by the sewing machine, learning to do the work with a Scholarshift curriculum. I am supposed to learn some skills completely new to me, and this is it.It reminds me of how I started my high school years at Shattuck-St. Mary’s. The cloth looks perfect, totally as strange as my new life in America.

My mother urged me to transfer here for better education from my junior high in Pinggu, a quiet district on the edge of Beijing. I never felt sure to leave. Nevertheless, I am here, surround by all English conversations that I can barely follow, and by this machine. Luckily, I am not a person easy to give up. I had to my new life, and make it fine, as I have to begin with the fabric.Of course I begin with making mistakes everywhere possible. I measure wrong. I cut too short, and have to patch up again.

Every failed stitch feels exactly like my first months here. I thought I spoke English well, but even the simplest greetings in little talks can now confuse me. Mostly, I stayed silent, thinking only to avoid mistakes.Fortunately, I never tend to turn away, but to face it head on: stitching this new life together one thread after another. I gradually learn to unpick wrong seams and go again, just like I began putting in extra hours to review my class notes every night and constantly visiting my teachers’ office hours. I asked them again and again to slow down their explanations and examples, and I finally started to catch up with the pace.

I start joining clubs and activities, and getting to know people and making friends. Every small step, every stitch, makes the whole picture a little clearer at a time.Sewing is now teaching me something books could never have. I used to chase results, as in China, numbers on the transcript define a student. In sewing, however, rushing only leads to misdoings. I must put faith in the process, measuring carefully, holding steadily, and stitching it right. The methodology spreads beyond sewing. Whether it’s programming or history in my second language, I stop regarding to them as some results to chase. Instead, they are something to patch together stitch by stitch.I finally finish my pajama pants. They are not fine ones at all. I haven’t left enough room at the crotch, and some stitches are wobbled.

But I hand-made them, and the imperfections feel like growth for the first time in my life instead of some failure sense.My stitching methodology is also how I take challenges. As a person who used to fear public speech, I joined the school choir. I spent two weeks learning to roll my tongue properly when singing. I’m really worried my East-Asian accent may make my voices sound so strange, but I listen, practice and conquer.After a couple of performances at the chapel, I even get recommended to give a speech there. The next scene of the story is me standing behind the podium.

I encourage the audience to turn fears into strength in English, the same second language that once silenced me.As I finished and the applause hit me, I thought of the piece of fabric that, for a moment, looked impossible to begin with. The pajama pants are still stored in my closet, uneven and slightly faded. It marks how “stitch” has turned from my favorite cartoon character to a noun that came with great pressure, denial and anxiety, and eventually to my methodology of persistence. Every crooked line marks a time when I don’t give up. Every patch marks when I learn to make corrections and progress.
